How Much Rent Can You Really Afford in 2026?

Landlords use simple math. Your budget should use real money. This guide covers the 30% rule and the 40 times rent check. Plus utilities, insurance, and roommates. With real examples. Swap in your pay stubs.

Quick math: If you earn $6,000 a month before tax, your rent ceiling is about $1,800 a month. (That's 30% of gross.) Most landlords phrase it as the 40 times rule: your yearly gross must be at least 40 times your monthly rent. Your take-home is closer to $4,400 a month after tax. So 30% of take-home is $1,320. That's the safer personal cap. Even if a landlord would approve more.

Two rules people actually use

Rule 1: 30% of income on housing

Gross version (for landlord screening): Take your monthly pay before tax. Multiply by 0.30. That's your rent ceiling.

Net version (for your bank): Take your actual take-home pay. Multiply by 0.30. If that's lower than the gross version, that's the one to trust. It's what keeps you from feeling house-poor.

Rule 2: 40 times rent on yearly salary

Some leases ask: is your yearly gross income at least 40 times the monthly rent?

Quick example: Rent is $2,000 a month. So 40 × $2,000 = $80,000 a year in gross income to pass.

Why this matches the 30% rule: $80,000 a year is about $6,667 a month before tax. $2,000 rent is 30% of that. Same math. Different words.

What this means for you

The gap between the gross rule and the net rule is $480 a month at a $72,000 salary. That's $5,800 a year. The landlord may say yes at the higher rent. But your bank account will say no.

Trust the take-home number. Not the gross one. Your rent should leave you room to save. To pay down debt. And to enjoy your life.

Costs beyond the listing price

The rent on the listing is almost never your full housing cost.

+ Utilities: Electric, gas, and water. Often $100 to $250 a month combined. Depending on climate and unit size.
+ Renter's insurance: Usually $15 to $30 a month. Cheap. And worth it.
+ Parking, pet, storage: Add-ons can be $50 to $300 a month. Read the lease.

Real example: Base rent is $1,800. Add $180 for utilities. $25 for insurance. $75 for parking. Your all-in housing is $2,080 a month. If your take-home is $4,400, that's 47% of your take-home. That's too high for most people. Even though the base rent alone was only 41%.

Roommates: your share vs the household's total

If you split a lease, the landlord cares about combined income. You care about your share.

Example: A $2,400 two-bedroom. Needs $96,000 combined gross to pass the 40 times rule. Two roommates earning $48,000 each pass that test. Each pays $1,200 a month. That's 30% of $4,000 monthly gross. But each person should still pass the take-home test on their own. Use our roommate budget planner for fair splits. Or read can I afford to live alone? for the solo math.

Frequently asked questions

Short answers to common questions. Pair them with your real pay stubs.

How much rent can I afford on my salary?

Start with 30% of your monthly gross. That's the landlord rule. Then check 30% of your take-home pay. Use the lower number. So you have room for savings, debt, and surprise bills.

Is rent 30% of gross or net income?

Gross is for landlord screening. Net (your take-home) is what feels affordable in real life. If the two numbers disagree, trust your take-home for your budget. And use gross only to check what a landlord will approve.

What is the 40 times rent rule?

Your yearly gross should be at least 40 times the monthly rent. Example: $1,500 rent needs $60,000 a year. It's the same idea as the 30% of gross rule. Just written differently.

Should I include utilities in the 30% rule?

It depends. To be safe, track rent + utilities + insurance as one number. Then see what percent of your take-home it is. If it's much more than 30%, you are carrying more house than your budget can hold.

Can I afford to live alone?

Yes. If your solo rent and utilities fit your take-home. With savings room. The landlord uses gross income. But your bank account runs on net. Run both tests. See can I afford to live alone for the solo math.

How We Calculate Affordability

  • The 28% rule: monthly rent at or below 28% of gross income.
  • Net-pay variant: 30% of take-home pay (after tax). We show both.
  • Renters insurance: flat $15/mo US median.
  • Utilities not included — add $150–$300/mo depending on the unit.

Data Sources

  • BLS CPI rent index — long-run rent inflation.
  • Zillow Research — metro rent (ZORI).
  • HUD Fair Market Rents — metro rent benchmarks.

What This Isn't

A rental application or a landlord pre-qualification. Many landlords require gross income of 2.5× to 3× monthly rent — a stricter test than the 28% rule.
