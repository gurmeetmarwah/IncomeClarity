Why $10,000 debt feels different from smaller balances

Same formulas—bigger dollars and longer timelines.

Interest charges scale up

At 22% APR, finance charges on a $10,000 balance run about $180+ per month early on. A $200 payment barely touches principal; many households need $350–$500+ fixed payments to make steady progress.

Minimum payments stretch for years

Issuer minimum formulas shrink as the balance falls, which can keep a $10k balance alive for well over a decade if you never pay extra. See why paying the minimum is costly.

Not the same as a $5k plan

Doubling the balance does not mean doubling the payment—interest grows faster than principal early on. Compare timelines on our $5,000 payoff page if you are deciding which balance to attack first.

Frequently asked questions — $10,000 debt

How long does it take to pay off $10k debt?

With 22% APR and $400/month, many models land near 4–5 years and roughly $3,000 in interest. Minimum-only paths can run much longer. Enter your issuer APR and a payment you can sustain in the planner above.

How much interest will I pay on $10,000?

Total interest is the sum of all finance charges until the balance hits zero. On $10k at typical card APRs, it is often thousands of dollars—sometimes more than half your payments in the early years. The payment comparison table shows interest at several payment levels.

Is $400 a month enough for $10,000?

At many APRs, $400/month is a reasonable fixed target—it usually beats minimums by a wide margin. If the planner shows “will not pay off,” your payment is below monthly interest; raise payment or seek a lower rate.

Should I pay off $10k before investing?

High-APR revolving debt often costs more than long-run investment averages after tax—many people prioritize it after a small cash buffer. Your job stability and employer match matter; this page models payoff math only, not investment advice.

How is this different from the $5,000 page?

This page defaults to $10,000 and higher example payments ($400 vs $200). The $5,000 payoff calculator uses smaller benchmarks—pick the page that matches your actual balance.
